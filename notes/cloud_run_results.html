ibm_kingston Persistence Tests

Six qubits of IBM's ibm_kingston processor were read out thousands of times in a row to look for a hidden variable that persists between measurements, in two models built for this project. Neither model shows a signal. The ordinary effects the runs uncovered, leakage and readout memory, matter for error correction.

What the hardware showed
Leakage and readout memory

Leakage episodes lasting tens of microseconds, driven by elapsed time rather than readouts, plus readout memory of either sign on four qubits.

What was tested

Donadi and Hossenfelder's 2022 toy model picks each measurement outcome by comparing a hidden random number with the Born probability, but it says nothing about how that number changes between measurements. A draft theory note in this project adds two minimal completions: the hidden variable belongs either to the detector or to the qubit, and survives from one readout to the next with probability w. Both keep every single readout at its Born value, so the only signature is a correlation between successive outcomes.

These completions are constructions made for this project, not proposals by Donadi or Hossenfelder. The runs bound these two models on this processor and readout. They do not test superdeterminism in general or Hossenfelder's 2025 collapse model.

How the runs worked

  • Each qubit ran a chain of 16 to 66 readouts per shot with no resets in between. Every setting was a frame phase on the same two π/2 pulses, so ordinary physics can only produce smooth, low-order dependence on the preparation angle.
  • Random relabeling of each readout separates detector-carried effects from qubit-carried ones, and a scan of the preparation angle looks for the cusp that a threshold hidden variable produces.
  • The design and the analysis code were committed before any data existed, for the main run and again for the follow-up.
  • QPU time charged: 12 s for the pilot, 2 s for a first main-run attempt that IBM rejected before running, 388 s for the main run and 178 s for the leak-tagged follow-up. That is 580 s of the free plan's 600 s per 28 days.

Leakage explains the shot-wide correlation

In the main run, neighboring readouts were correlated on every qubit, and the correlation stayed nearly as strong two to eight readouts apart. A hidden variable that persists from readout to readout cannot produce that pattern. The follow-up placed two flip pulses after every four readouts: a qubit that has leaked out of its two levels reads "1" three times in a row instead of alternating, which tags the block.

The leaked fraction barely changed when the readout rate dropped by a factor of 3.8, so the leakage builds up with elapsed time rather than being caused by the readouts. Lifetimes from the tags are 9 to 124 µs; these are lower limits.

Why it matters beyond the models

Leakage that lasts for many readout rounds and readout memory at the 10−3 level are the kinds of correlated measurement errors that degrade surface-code memory in simulations by Kam and colleagues (Quantum Sci. Technol., 2025). Seeing them took about 107 readout pairs per qubit, well within a free cloud allowance.
